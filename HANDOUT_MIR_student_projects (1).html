Music information retrieval: semester projects

How to read a card

Each card has the same parts. The question says what the project decides. The starting point says which published work it builds on. The first steps are what a team does in its first days. The plan lists the stages in order. The result either way says what the team hands in if the hypothesis holds and if it fails. The line "what would refute it" names the outcome that ends the project early, and the team writes that outcome down before running anything at scale. The reading list gives the papers to read first; every one is on arXiv under the identifier shown.

Teams can use coding agents. The projects are chosen so that an agent can write the scripts but cannot produce the result: each needs data that does not exist yet, long runs, or judgment calls the team must defend. Three rules apply. Every number in the paper comes from a file the team produced. Every cited paper has been read by a team member. The prompts and agent sessions that produced code are kept in the repository.

Compute is marked on each card: no GPU, or one workstation with a consumer GPU. A project marked "human study" needs ethics approval before data collection and a credit for every participant; the card says what the study is in one sentence.

S1
What do audio-language judges of music use? Probes with the audio removed, swapped, stripped of vocals and replaced
An audio-language model prompted to rate a music clip is called a judge. Judges are validated by how well their ratings correlate with human ratings, and nothing in that number says what the judge used. This project builds a set of probes, test conditions in which one input is changed and the rating should or should not change, and runs them on open judges, on learned scorers (networks trained to predict human ratings from the audio and the prompt) and on one commercial judge at a fixed version. On one probe, a clip played under the wrong prompt, it compares every judge with what human raters do.
evaluationcompute: one workstation GPUhuman study
open the plan
The question
How much of a judge's agreement with human ratings survives when the audio is removed, when the clip is replaced by another generator's clip for the same prompt, when the order of two clips is swapped, when the vocals are separated out, and when an irrelevant clip replaces the right one? Do learned scorers, which were trained on the same human ratings, show the same effects?
Starting point
A speech-evaluation audit found that audio-language judges take shortcuts through the protocol; one judge, for example, picked the same position in a pair of clips whichever clip was placed there. On SongEval, a rating set of full songs, a learned scorer's scores were shown to follow how close a song is to pop music. A NeurIPS 2026 paper showed that an irrelevant image changes the labels vision-language judges give to text. These findings have not been put into one protocol for music, and no study we read compares a judge's behavior under a swapped clip with the behavior of human raters under the same swap.
First steps
  1. Run one open judge on 50 MusicEval items (generated clips with expert ratings of quality and of how well the clip matches its prompt) and compute its correlation with the expert ratings.
  2. Repeat the run with the audio removed from the input, so that the judge sees only the prompt; the size of that gap sets the scale for everything else.
  3. Write down the result that would refute the project.
Plan
  1. Build the probe set. The no-audio probe removes the clip. The same-prompt swap replaces the clip with another generator's clip for the same prompt, so that a score that follows the clip played is a score of the audio and a score that follows the replaced clip's expert rating is a score of the prompt. The order swap applies to pairwise judging, where the judge sees two clips and picks the better one: every pair is shown in both orders. The genre mix varies the genre of the prompts. The vocal probe removes the singing by source separation, a model that splits a song into voice and accompaniment, on SongEval. The lyric probe masks the lyrics in the prompt. The irrelevant-clip probe shows 200 text-only labeling items with a clip that matches the text, a clip that contradicts it, or no clip.
  2. Run three open judges, two learned scorers and one commercial judge through every probe with confidence intervals from the bootstrap: resample the items with replacement and recompute each statistic.
  3. Bin the order-swap pairs by the gap between the two clips' expert ratings and report the flip rate (the share of pairs whose preferred clip changes with the order) per bin, so the paper says whether the order effect shrinks when that gap is wide.
  4. Run the human control: three listeners rate 50 same-prompt swaps under the instructions the judges receive, so that a judge that follows the prompt is compared with what people do.
  5. Report per judge the share of agreement that needs the audio, whether the score follows the clip or the prompt, the order effect by margin, the share that comes from the lyrics, and the share of text labels that an irrelevant clip changes; report the same rows for the learned scorers.
  6. Write the paper and release the probe set as a reusable test.
Result either way
Judges that need the audio, follow the clip under the swap, and are stable under the other probes are validated for use on songs. Judges that are not give the paper a table of what to control. If a learned scorer follows the prompt under the swap, the ratings it was trained on carry the prompt into the scorer, and every paper that trains on those ratings must report it. The probe set becomes the check anyone runs before trusting a judge.
What would refute it
Every judge and scorer keeps under 20% of its agreement without audio, follows the clip under the same-prompt swap, shows no order or vocal effect at any margin, and changes under 5% of text labels under a contradicting clip.
Paper
ISMIR 2027; NLP4MusA; a workshop on evaluation at ICML, NeurIPS or ACL 2027.
Needs
MusicEval and SongEval ratings (public), one open source-separation model, open audio-language models, two learned scorers that take the prompt and the audio, access to one commercial model through an interface, a corpus with genre tags such as the Free Music Archive, and three listeners for the human control.
Compute
one workstation GPU
Human study
A small rating task in which three listeners rate 50 clips played under the wrong prompt, with the same instructions the judges receive; no personal data is collected beyond the ratings.
Main risk
Open judges may be weak overall, which makes every probe look like a null result. Include a learned scorer with published correlations as the reference. The same-prompt swap needs prompts that at least two generators share; MusicEval has them and SongEval may not, so the swap is reported on MusicEval alone if needed.

S2
What a language model learns about pitch from MIDI tokens: initialization, probes, steering and prompting
A text language model can be taught symbolic music by adding MIDI tokens to its vocabulary. MIDI is a format that records notes as events (which pitch, when, how loud), and a token is one unit in the model's vocabulary, so a MIDI token stands for one such event or for part of one. The new tokens start from an initialization, the values their embeddings hold before training, and the choice is open. Whether the trained model then lays pitch out in a structure it uses is also open. This project trains small models with four initializations and tests the result with probes (small models fitted to the network's activations, the numbers each layer outputs, to read out a property such as pitch) and with steering (adding a direction to the activations to change the output). It then compares steering against the plain alternative, asking for the pitch in the prompt, and against a random direction of the same length.
modelscompute: one workstation GPU
open the plan
The question
Does the initialization of new MIDI tokens change what a small language model learns at a fixed training budget; does the model use the pitch structure that probes find; and does steering along that structure move the output more than a prompt that asks for the same pitch and more than a random direction of the same length?
Starting point
Studies of vocabulary expansion for new languages compare initializations. An ISMIR 2026 paper found pitch laid out as a helix (a spiral in which notes an octave apart sit above each other) in audio foundation models. Open text-to-MIDI models release their checkpoints, the saved weights of a trained model. A 2026 study of text models found that steering can work at layers where the logit lens (reading the model's prediction at each layer) decodes nothing. No study we read combines these on symbolic music, and no steering study we read on a music model reports a prompting baseline, a split-half check and a random direction of the same length together.
First steps
  1. Append a MIDI token block to a small open model and train for one hour with random initialization; confirm that the training loss falls.
  2. Fit the helix probe to one released text-to-MIDI model at one layer and record how well a helix fits against a shuffled control.
  3. Steer one released model toward one pitch class (the note name without its octave, such as C or F sharp) and read the pitch classes in 50 generations against a prompt that asks for the same note.
  4. Write down the result that would refute the project.
Plan
  1. Four initializations: random; the mean of the existing embeddings; from a descriptor, a short text written per token family (for example 'a note starts') and encoded by the model; from a model that decodes MIDI natively. Three seeds each (a seed fixes the random numbers so that a run can be repeated), and one fixed token budget (the total number of training tokens, the same for every run).
  2. Training loss at budget and, with a logit lens, the layer at which the new tokens bind, the first layer whose prediction names a MIDI token.
  3. The helix probe per layer on the trained models and on two released models under two tokenizations (two ways of turning a MIDI file into tokens, for example one that writes a time-shift token and one that writes a bar and a position token).
  4. The steering comparison across four conditions: a prompt that asks for the target pitch class; a steering direction found on one half of the data and tested on the other half (the split-half check); a random direction of the same length as the steering direction (the matched-norm null); and prompt and steering combined. The readout is the share of generated notes in the target pitch class and a measure of how much the rest of the music changed.
  5. The lens-against-steering table: for each layer, whether the logit lens decodes the target pitch class and whether steering at that layer moves it, so that the two can disagree.
  6. Write the paper: whether initialization matters, whether the geometry is used, and which controls a steering claim on a music model needs.
Result either way
A difference beyond the seed spread tells practitioners how to initialize. No difference is a negative result that saves large runs. Steering that moves pitch classes more than the prompt and more than the random direction gives a control; steering that does not beat the prompt shows a structure the model carries without using, and a prompt that does the same job at no cost. A layer where steering works and the lens decodes nothing repeats the text-model finding on music.
What would refute it
Loss at budget and binding depth do not differ between initializations beyond the seed spread, and rotation within the helix plane moves the generated pitch classes no more than the prompt and no more than a random direction of the same length.
Paper
ISMIR 2027; an interpretability or tokenization workshop at ICML or NeurIPS 2027.
Needs
A small open language model, MidiTok (a library of MIDI tokenizers), the Lakh MIDI dataset, the released MIDI-LLM and text2midi checkpoints, and one workstation GPU for tens of hours.
Compute
one workstation GPU
Human study
None. The team's own listening is a check, not a study.
Main risk
Tens of GPU hours per run limit the seeds. Fix the budget first and never compare runs of different budgets. Steering that changes the pitch class by making the music worse is a failure; report a quality measure next to every steering number.

S3
Minimal pairs for music-text models, in the words people use
A minimal pair is two captions or two clips that differ in exactly one musical attribute, a property such as the instrument, the key, one changed note, a negation ('no drums'), or the order of two events. Pairs show what a model encodes and what it ignores. This project first collects how people describe music, builds the pair families from those words, and tests whether joint audio-text models (models that map captions and clips into one embedding space, so that a caption can be scored against a clip) pick the clip that matches the caption. Each pair is scored in two wordings, the words people used and the words caption pipelines (automatic procedures that write captions from tags or metadata) use, and beside the share of listeners who tell the pair apart.
datasetcompute: one workstation GPUhuman study
open the plan
The question
Which musical attributes that people name in free descriptions can joint audio-text models tell apart, which do they miss, and does the wording of the caption change the answer as much as the attribute does?
Starting point
A 2025 study found that joint audio-text embeddings capture timbre words (words for the color of a sound, such as 'bright' or 'warm') only partially. A 2026 alignment paper builds hard negatives, wrong captions that differ from the right one by a pitch change. A cross-cultural study of how people describe generated music shows that free descriptions use words that caption pipelines do not. No benchmark we read builds pairs from the words people use, reports a baseline that needs no model, or reports how many listeners can hear each difference.
First steps
  1. Collect 30 free descriptions of 10 clips from course participants and list the attributes they mention.
  2. Render 20 pairs of one attribute family (for example, transposition: the same passage played in a different key) from MIDI and check with your own ears that only one attribute changed.
  3. Score them with one CLAP model, a joint audio-text model trained on captions, in two wordings of the caption; write down the result that would refute the project.
Plan
  1. Collect 300 descriptions of 30 clips; compare their attributes and vocabulary with three caption pipelines on the same clips (human captions, captions a language model writes from tags, captions rendered from metadata).
  2. Choose five attribute families from that comparison and render 200 to 500 controlled pairs per family; for two families, add pairs from real recordings (two takes of one piece in different keys, or with and without a vocal part), so that rendered pairs can be compared with recorded ones.
  3. Write each pair's captions in two wordings, the participants' words and the pipelines' words.
  4. Run the listening check: for every family, listeners choose the matching clip for 20 pairs; the share correct is the human ceiling for that family, the best score a model can be expected to reach.
  5. Score CLAP variants, CLaMP 3 and one music-language model against a parameter-free baseline (a method with no trained weights that matches caption keywords to the outputs of an automatic tagger), per family, per wording and per source (rendered or recorded).
  6. Release the descriptions and the pairs, and write the paper.
Result either way
Families at chance show what the models do not encode, provided listeners tell the pairs apart. Families above 90% show where the models can be trusted. A wording effect as large as the family effect means the benchmark must report both wordings. The description study is a result on its own: what people mention that pipelines do not, and the reverse.
What would refute it
Every model exceeds 90% on every family in both wordings and on both sources, and human and pipeline vocabularies overlap above 0.8 on every attribute family.
Paper
ISMIR 2027; NLP4MusA; the late-breaking work track of CHI 2028 for the description study.
Needs
CLAP checkpoints, CLaMP 3, a MIDI renderer with several instruments, the Song Describer dataset, a small set of recordings with two takes of one piece, an automatic tagger, credit for the participants who write descriptions, and listeners for the check.
Compute
one workstation GPU
Human study
A study in which listeners write 300 free descriptions of 30 clips, and a listening check in which listeners choose the matching clip for 20 pairs per family, so that each family has a human ceiling; no personal data is collected beyond the texts and the choices.
Main risk
Hand-made pairs can leak a second difference. Keep a listening check on every family before scoring. Recorded pairs are hard to find for most families; two families with recorded pairs are enough for the comparison.

S4
Copies and sameness: memorization onset and diversity in open music generators
Two questions about what a music generator outputs have the same tools. Does it copy its training songs (memorization)? Do several generators, given the same prompt, produce the same music (homogeneity)? This project plants known near-copies, training clips reproduced almost exactly, into a small generator's outputs to compare two copy detectors, and measures within-model and across-model similarity for several open generators against human music that fits the prompts. It adds a time axis: the small generator is fine-tuned (trained further on a small set of tracks) at two training-set sizes with saved checkpoints, the weights stored during training, so that the project can say when copying begins and whether a collection-level test finds the training set before any copy is audible.
evaluationcompute: one workstation GPUhuman study
open the plan
The question
Does a version-identification model (trained to recognize another performance of the same song) find planted copies that an embedding distance misses; when during fine-tuning do copies appear, and does that time depend on the training-set size; and do open generators converge on the same outputs more than they converge on human music?
Starting point
Dataset inference attacks on audio generators, tests that decide from a collection of samples whether the collection was in the training set, rely on embedding similarity, and a NeurIPS 2025 paper reports that the per-sample version of the test is weak on large models. A 2025 study of image diffusion models found that memorization starts at a training time that grows with the training-set size. Version identification, the task of recognizing another performance of the same song, has a unified benchmark since 2026. A 2025 study found open-ended homogeneity across language models; no study we read measures it for music generators, and no study we read puts memorization onset and a collection-level test on the same audio checkpoints.
First steps
  1. Fine-tune a small open generator on 200 licensed tracks, saving a checkpoint every few hundred steps, and plant ten near-copies among its outputs; score them with one CLAP model (an encoder that maps audio to an embedding, so that a copy should sit at a small embedding distance from its source).
  2. Generate 50 clips from the same prompt with two generators and compute their pairwise embedding distances.
  3. Write down the result that would refute the project.
Plan
  1. Planted copies at several lengths; detection rate per detector (embedding similarity against version identification) and per length.
  2. The onset study: the same generator fine-tuned on 200 and on 800 tracks with checkpoints, three seeds (a seed fixes the random numbers so that a run can be repeated). At each checkpoint: the memorization fraction (the share of outputs whose nearest training clip is closer than a fixed threshold), the two copy detectors, and a collection-level dataset-inference test that compares the training tracks with held-out tracks of the same source.
  3. Generation from one prompt set with three or more open generators; within-model and across-model similarity per category and per embedding space, against human references for the same prompts, with human-to-human similarity as the floor.
  4. A listening check in which listeners rate the similarity of clip pairs of three kinds (same model, two models, model and human), so that the embedding result is confirmed or contradicted by ear.
  5. Write the paper with the tables, the recommendation of a copy detector, and a stopping rule for fine-tuning.
Result either way
If version identification finds more copies, memorization studies should switch to it; if not, embedding similarity is confirmed. An onset that moves with the set size gives a stopping rule for fine-tuning; a collection-level test that fires before any copy is audible separates the two questions. Convergence across generators is a finding about diversity; its absence contrasts with the language-model result.
What would refute it
Both detectors find planted copies at the same rate, the memorization fraction rises at the same checkpoint for both set sizes within the seed spread, and across-model similarity is below model-to-human similarity in every category and every space.
Paper
ISMIR 2027; DAFx 2027; DCASE 2027 workshop.
Needs
A small open generator that fine-tunes on one GPU, version-identification models, 1,000 licensed tracks, open generators (MusicGen, Stable Audio Open, ACE-Step), open encoders, and listeners for the two checks.
Compute
one workstation GPU
Human study
A listening check in which listeners confirm that planted near-copies are audible as copies, and rate the similarity of clip pairs from the same model, from two models, and from a model and a human reference.
Main risk
Fine-tuning may fail to produce audible copies. Start with short, high-amplitude plants and shorten them. The onset study doubles the GPU time; fix the checkpoint interval and the two set sizes before the first run.

S5
Audio watermarks and dataset marks under attack: removal, forging, and the mark a generator has learned
An audio watermark is a hidden signal embedded in a clip to mark it. A dataset mark is a change applied to a whole dataset so that a model trained on it can be identified later. In audio the mark is a faint delayed copy of the sound added to each recording (an echo, like a short reverb), which a model trained on the data reproduces in its outputs; the audit reads the echo from those outputs with a cepstrum, a transform in which an echo shows up as a peak at its delay. This project builds an attack suite from the audio tools ISMIR 2026 papers released (codec resynthesis, decoding a clip from a neural codec's tokens; latent shifts, moving the clip's representation inside the codec before decoding; effect chains, the compression and equalization a producer applies; re-recording through a loudspeaker) and tests three things: whether the tools remove the marks at acceptable quality; whether an attacker can forge a dataset mark onto clean audio so that it passes the audit; and whether a mark that a small generator has learned from marked training data survives attacks on the generator's outputs and partial cleaning of its training data.
securitycompute: one workstation GPUhuman study
open the plan
The question
Which released audio tools remove a watermark or a dataset mark while keeping the audio quality; can clean audio be made to pass an echo detector without access to the detector; and does an echo that a small generator learned from its training data survive when its outputs are attacked or when part of its training data is cleaned?
Starting point
Training-free structural removal of neural audio watermarks was shown in 2026. Echo marks were shown to survive training of instrument models, and a 2026 learned speech watermark survives fine-tuning. Black-box forging of watermarks (forging without access to the detector's internals) exists for images; no paper we read tests it on audio dataset marks. Every removal paper we read attacks the clip; no paper we read attacks the outputs or the training data of a generator that has already learned the mark.
First steps
  1. Reproduce echo detection on ten tracks with a cepstral detector.
  2. Implement the simplest attack, a codec round trip (compressing the audio and decompressing it), and plot detection against bitrate (the amount of data per second the codec keeps).
  3. Run the detector on 100 clean clips that contain sustained notes and read how often it fires without a mark.
  4. Write down the result that would refute the project.
Plan
  1. The attack suite: every released tool as a function from clip to clip, with ViSQOL (a tool that scores audio quality against a reference) on each output.
  2. Detection against quality per attack for AudioSeal (a public neural watermark) and for the echo mark on clips, and the false-positive rate of the echo detector on clean clips with sustained notes whose period equals the echo delay (the pitch confound).
  3. The model arm: a small audio-to-audio generator fine-tuned on marked data with the echo in 100%, 50% and 25% of the training clips (the coverage), and on clean data as the control; the audit on its outputs before and after each attack from the suite. Before the results are read, the threat model, the list of what the attacker can reach (the outputs, the training data, or both), is written down.
  4. The forger: estimate the echo delay from marked clips, apply it to clean clips, and measure the pass rate (the share of forged clips the audit accepts as marked) against the echo's amplitude and the audio quality.
  5. The listening check with eight listeners on attacked and forged clips.
  6. Write the paper and release the suite.
Result either way
Marks that survive support their robustness claims. Marks that are removed or forged at inaudible amplitudes define the threat model and say whether an audit needs a secret key, a parameter such as the delay that only the auditor knows. A learned mark that survives output attacks that remove the clip mark shows where the audit is stronger than the watermark; a mark that disappears when a quarter of the training data is cleaned sets the coverage an audit needs.
What would refute it
Mark detection stays above 90% under every attack at matched quality on clips and on the generator's outputs, the detector never fires on clean clips with sustained notes, and forged clips never pass the audit at an amplitude listeners rate as acceptable.
Paper
DAFx 2027; DCASE 2027 workshop; IEEE WIFS 2027 for the forging result.
Needs
AudioSeal, the released codec checkpoints, a small audio-to-audio generator that trains on one GPU (DDSP or RAVE), public audio such as the Free Music Archive, ViSQOL, and eight listeners.
Compute
one workstation GPU
Human study
A small listening check in which eight listeners rate the quality of attacked and forged clips.
Main risk
Attacks may destroy quality. Fix the lowest acceptable quality first and report only attacks above it. The fine-tuning runs need days of GPU time; fix the three coverage levels and one architecture before the first run.

S6
Ghosts in the encoder: artist identity inside a text-to-song model, what it predicts about copying, and whether it can be erased
A text-to-song model can be prompted to imitate a named artist and sometimes reproduces whole songs from its training data. A 2026 study found that such a model carries a linearly decodable representation of artist identity from the lyrics alone, with no artist name given: a small classifier (a probe) trained on the model's activations can tell which artist a song is by. This project asks whether that representation predicts copying, and whether it can be removed. Copying is measured with an exact test: instead of reading a similarity score against a threshold, the test compares training items with held-out items that were never seen, so that chance matches do not count.
modelscompute: one workstation GPU
open the plan
The question
Do artists whose identity a probe decodes most reliably also have the most training songs reproduced, does erasing the identity direction from the activations reduce imitation at a small cost in quality, and does the direction survive when the model is accelerated?
Starting point
The artist probe was shown on ACE-Step with 2,000 songs across 100 artists. Exact tests for memorization with false-discovery control were published for image models in 2026. Closed-form linear concept erasure (removing from activations everything a linear probe could read about a concept) exists for text models. Nobody we read has tied the probe to copying under an exact null, or tried the erasure on a music model.
First steps
  1. Reproduce the artist probe on ACE-Step for 20 artists with the released weights and public lyrics.
  2. Compute a copy score for 50 generated songs per artist against that artist's training songs and against held-out songs; read whether the two distributions differ.
  3. Write down the result that would refute the project.
Plan
  1. Probe accuracy and regurgitation rate under the exact null for 50 artists; the rank correlation between the two.
  2. Linear erasure of the artist direction at one layer; imitation measured with a verification-style embedding (how close generated songs sit to the artist's real songs), and quality with a judge and a short listening check; both before and after erasure.
  3. The same probe on an accelerated version of the model, if one is released, to see whether acceleration removes the direction.
  4. The paper: the correlation, the erasure trade-off, and a recommendation for model builders.
Result either way
A correlation plus an erasure that halves imitation at small cost gives model builders a tool and the field a mechanism for imitation. A null correlation or an inert direction says the decodable identity does not drive copying, which the interpretability field would still publish.
What would refute it
Probe accuracy does not predict regurgitation under the exact null, and erasing the direction leaves imitation unchanged.
Paper
ISMIR 2027; NeurIPS 2027 or an interpretability workshop.

What does fine-tuning change inside a music model? Model diffing with sparse autoencoders and crosscoders
When a text language model is taught music, for example by adding MIDI tokens to its vocabulary and training it on symbolic music, something inside it changes. Model diffing is the set of methods that find what changed by comparing the model before and after. One tool is a sparse autoencoder, a small network trained on a model's internal activations that splits them into a short list of features, each of which fires on one recognizable thing (a pitch class, a drum pattern, a key). A crosscoder is the same idea trained on two models at once, so that each feature is tagged by whether it belongs to the base model, the fine-tuned model, or both. S8
Which statistical test detects a dataset mark in a generator's outputs, and how many generations does it need?
A dataset mark is a change planted in training data so that a model trained on that data can be recognized later from its outputs. In audio the mark is a faint echo, a delayed copy of the sound like a short reverb. An audit scores the model's outputs for the mark: each generated clip gets an echo score, the height of the clip's cepstrum (a transform in which an echo shows up as a peak at its delay) at the echo's delay divided by the spread of the cepstrum at nearby delays, which makes it a z-score. The audit's decision rests on a statistical test over those scores. The test asks whether the scores follow the null distribution, the distribution of scores expected from a model that never saw the mark, and the auditor must build that null before testing. The choice of test and the choice of null decide how many generations the auditor must collect. This project compares eight goodness-of-fit tests (tests of whether observed scores follow the null distribution) and a sequential test, which re-examines the decision after every new generation and can stop early. It runs them first on synthetic score distributions, then on the outputs of a small generator fine-tuned on marked data (fine-tuning continues the training of a released model on new data), under two nulls and two shares of marked training data.

open the plan
The question

Starting point
A NeurIPS 2025 paper compared eight goodness-of-fit tests for text watermark detection from per-token statistics. A 2026 paper proposed e-processes, sequential tests that stay valid whenever the auditor chooses to stop, for online text watermark detection. What can a music-generation leaderboard claim? Metric instability and the limits of rating sets
Generated music is ranked by two kinds of number. The first is a distance between the embeddings of generated audio and of a reference set, a set of real recordings the generated audio is compared with. FAD and KAD are two such distances: FAD compares the mean and the covariance of the two sets of embeddings, and KAD compares the two sets through a kernel, a function that scores the similarity of every pair of points. The second kind of number is a human rating collected on a fixed set of prompts, where a prompt is the text the generator was asked to turn into music. This project measures how much a ranking depends on choices that papers rarely report: the embedding model, the layer of that model the embedding is taken from, the reference set, the design of the rating set, and the protocol under which the raters worked.
evaluationcompute: one workstation GPUhuman study
open the plan
The question
How many published rankings of music generators survive a change of the embedding model, its layer or the reference set? How much of a human rating is explained by the prompt rather than by the audio, and does a protocol in which listeners compare two clips of the same generator separate the generator from the clip better than ratings of single clips do?
Starting point
Several ISMIR 2026 generation papers released their outputs. A reference-free metric paper (a metric that scores audio without a reference set) reports that different reference sets give different trends, so the same generators can be ranked differently depending on the reference set. MusicEval holds expert ratings of 2,748 clips from 31 generators, 25 of which share one set of prompts, so part of every rating is the prompt rather than the audio. The raters rated each clip on its own. No public music rating set we read asked raters to compare two clips of the same generator, so these sets do not say how much of the agreement between raters is agreement about which generator made the clip.
First steps
  1. Reproduce one paper's FAD and KAD numbers from its released outputs within the tolerance the paper reports.
  2. Load the MusicEval ratings and fit a regression with group effects, a model in which each prompt and each generator gets its own additive term; read how much of the rating variance the prompt terms alone explain.
  3. Compute the agreement between raters on all clips and within one generator; the gap between the two numbers is the size of the question the listening study answers.
  4. Write down the result that would refute the project.
Plan

  2. Run it on at least eight papers with released outputs; count the pairwise orderings of generators that flip outside the intervals.

The prompt-only ceiling is the best generator-level agreement any scorer that reads only the prompt can reach, given that most generators share one prompt set. The agreement between raters is computed on all clips and again within one generator. The adjusted ranking is the ranking by the generator terms of the regression, with the prompt terms removed.
  4. Run the listening study: about twenty listeners rate 120 clips from six generators under two protocols, ratings of single clips and paired comparisons of two clips of the same generator for the same prompt; compare the agreement between listeners within a generator under each protocol.
  5. Write the paper: a table of papers against audit conditions, the adjusted leaderboard, the protocol comparison, and a one-page reporting checklist.
Result either way
If most orderings survive and the adjustment moves no rank, the paper certifies the practice and says which choices are safe. If orderings flip or ranks move, the paper says which choices must be reported and which leaderboard rows are not supported. The listening study says whether paired comparison raises agreement within a generator. If it does, rating sets should collect paired comparisons. If it does not, the low agreement within a generator is a property of the clips rather than of the protocol.

Fewer than two of eight audited papers show an ordering change outside the intervals, the prompt effect explains under 5% of rating variance, and the paired protocol raises agreement within a generator by less than 0.05 in rank correlation.

How much of a music benchmark can be solved without listening? Floors for retrieval and question answering
Music-text benchmarks ask a model to match captions to clips or to answer questions about a clip. Part of each benchmark can be solved from the text alone, and that part is a floor: the score a method reaches without listening. For example, a caption that says 'solo piano' can be matched to a clip tagged 'piano' without hearing it. This project computes the floor for five retrieval benchmarks (where a model ranks clips for a caption) and three question-answering benchmarks, measures the floor people reach without the audio, and releases the subset of questions that do need the audio.

open the plan
The question
What share of each benchmark's score can be reached without the audio, by a method with no learned parameters, by an audio-language model, and by people, and which items remain once that share is removed?
Starting point
A 2026 study showed that audio-language models keep 60 to 72% of their score on three audio benchmarks without any audio, because the questions can be answered from text priors, what a language model knows about the likely answer before hearing anything. Caption benchmarks render metadata (the tags, instruments and genres stored with a clip) as text, which a text matcher can exploit. No paper we read puts one floor beside the published scores of every music benchmark, and none we read reports what people score on the same items without the audio.
First steps
  1. Implement the parameter-free baseline (a method with no trained weights) on one retrieval benchmark: match the attributes named in the caption (the instrument, genre, tempo and mood words) against the clip's metadata rendered as text, and add BM25, a standard text search score that counts the words a caption and a clip's text share, weighted by how rare each word is.
  2. Run one open audio-language model on one question-answering benchmark with normal, silent and shuffled audio (the clip of another item); read the share of the score that survives.
  3. Write down the result that would refute the project.
Plan
  1. The retrieval floor on five benchmarks beside the best published model score, with a control that matches caption lengths, so that a long caption does not score higher only because it names more words.
  2. The question-answering runs with normal, silent and shuffled audio on three benchmarks and two models; the items answered without audio are flagged.
  3. The human floor: about fifteen listeners answer 150 flagged and unflagged items in two conditions, question only and question with audio; the share they answer correctly without audio is the human text prior, reported beside the model's.
  4. A listening check of the remaining items, and a released subset that needs the audio for both models and people.
  5. Write the paper: one table of floors (parameter-free, model without audio, people without audio) and one of audio-dependent shares, with the subset.
Result either way
High floors mean the benchmarks measure attribute recovery rather than listening, and the paper gives the fix. Low floors certify the benchmarks, which is also a result worth printing. If people also answer the flagged items without audio, the items are faulty. If people need the audio where models do not, the models have learned the distribution of answers, and that is a finding about the models rather than about the benchmark.
What would refute it
The floor is below one tenth of the best model's score on every retrieval benchmark, models keep under 20% of their score without audio on every question-answering benchmark, and people answer under 30% of the items correctly without audio.
Paper
NLP4MusA; ISMIR 2027 late-breaking.
Needs
The five retrieval benchmarks and three question-answering benchmarks (public), two open audio-language models, a web page for the listening study, and about fifteen listeners.
Compute
one workstation GPU
Human study
A listening study in which about fifteen listeners answer 150 benchmark questions twice, once from the text alone and once with the audio; no personal data is collected beyond the answers and a self-reported level of musical training.
Main risk
Benchmarks differ in metadata richness, so the floor may measure metadata rather than the benchmark. Report the floor with and without metadata fields. A listener who sees a question twice remembers the first answer; separate the two conditions by several days, or split the items between two groups.

When AI-music detectors fail: real music they mislabel, the same tracks re-processed, and songs with one generated half
An AI-music detector is a classifier that says whether a clip was generated. Two of its failures are unmeasured on music: false alarms on real music, and blindness to songs where only the lyrics or only the audio were generated. This project builds the two test sets and runs public detectors on them. It adds a third test in which the same real tracks are re-encoded through a codec (a program that compresses audio, such as MP3), re-leveled, edited and marked with a watermark (a hidden signal embedded in a clip to mark it), so that a change in the detector's answer can be traced to one cause.
datasetcompute: one workstation GPUhuman study
open the plan
The question
Which properties of real music (recording decade, vocal language, source, codec) make detectors raise false alarms; which of those effects remain when the same track is changed in one way at a time; and can lyric detectors and audio detectors tell apart a song with generated lyrics from a song with generated audio?
Starting point
ISMIR 2026 papers report decade and vocal-language effects on detection. A benchmark with known lineage of every track (which generator or recording it came from, and what was done to it since) showed that detectors fail under distribution shift, the case where the test data differ from the training data in ways the detector never saw. A speech study found that a detector trained where only the fake class carries a watermark flags watermarked real speech as fake; no paper we read tests this on music. Lyric-transcript detection and detection of generated stems in hybrid mixes each exist alone.
First steps
  1. Run one public detector on 200 real clips from two decades and read the false-positive rate per decade, the share of real clips the detector calls generated.
  2. Re-encode the same 200 clips through one codec at two bitrates (the amount of data per second the codec keeps) and read the change in the false-positive rate.
  3. Generate ten songs with custom human lyrics through an open song generator and transcribe them with a speech recognizer; run one lyric detector.
  4. Write down the result that would refute the project.
Plan
  1. Real-music strata, groups of recordings that share one property: three public detectors on recordings grouped by decade, vocal language and source (studio, live, broadcast, phone); false-positive rate per stratum with bandwidth (the highest frequency the recording keeps) as a covariate.
  2. The intervention set: the same real tracks in six versions (the original; a codec round trip at two bitrates; loudness normalized to one level; pitch-shifted or remixed as a listener might upload it; marked with a public audio watermark). The false-positive rate per version on the same tracks separates the effect of the recording from the effect of the processing.
  3. The four-cell set: human lyrics with human audio, generated lyrics with human audio, human lyrics with generated audio, both generated. Generated audio sings given lyrics through an open song generator; generated lyrics are sung by people or by a singing-voice model that takes a text.
  4. Detection scores per cell and per axis for lyric detectors and audio detectors, and where transcription (turning the sung words into text with a speech recognizer) breaks lyric detection, with the word error rate per cell.
  5. Write the paper and release both sets with their lineage.
Result either way
Flat false-positive rates certify the detectors on these axes; rates that vary give a list of controls. A rate that moves when the same track is re-encoded or watermarked names the processing step that a user must remove or report before trusting a verdict. Independent axes in the four-cell set make combined screening valid; interacting axes show where it breaks.
What would refute it
False-positive rates differ by fewer than 5 points across every stratum and across every version of the same tracks for every detector, and transcript-based detectors are at chance on every cell.
Paper
ISMIR 2027; DCASE 2027 workshop; NLP4MusA for the lyrics axis.
Needs
Three public detectors, open corpora of real music with metadata (the Free Music Archive and the live recordings of the Internet Archive), codec tools and a public audio watermark (AudioSeal), open song generators that accept custom lyrics, a speech recognizer, and three listeners.
Compute
one workstation GPU
Human study
A small check in which three listeners confirm, on a sample of the four-cell set, that the sung words match the given lyrics and that each clip is a song rather than noise.
Main risk
Metadata on decade and language may be noisy. Hand-check a sample of each stratum. Old recordings are also low in bandwidth, so a decade effect can be a bandwidth effect; the intervention set on the same tracks is the check.

S15
What guidance does to generated audio: the guidance scale, the null embedding, diversity, copying and what judges prefer
Text-to-audio diffusion models follow the prompt through classifier-free guidance: at every step the model mixes a prediction made with the prompt and one made without it, and a scale sets how far it leans toward the prompt. Higher scales give outputs that match the prompt better and, in images, outputs that look more alike and copy training data more often. The prompt-free branch is usually an empty, fixed vector; a 2026 speech paper replaces it with a learned one and reports more stable outputs at high scales. This project measures, for music and sound, what the scale and the choice of null do to diversity, copying, and the scores that automatic judges and listeners give.
evaluationcompute: one workstation GPU
open the plan
The question
How do within-prompt diversity, copy rate and judge preference change with the guidance scale on two text-to-audio models, does a learned null embedding change those curves, and do judges prefer a different scale than listeners do?
Starting point
Guidance is in every text-to-audio model's sampler and its scale is a reported hyperparameter chosen by eye. Learned null embeddings exist for speech. Low-cost guidance in the latent space exists for audio. No paper we read reports the diversity, copying and judge-preference curves for audio generation, or a listener check of the scale judges prefer.
First steps
  1. Generate 10 clips for each of 200 prompts with Stable Audio Open at guidance scales 1, 3, 5, 7 and 9; compute within-prompt diversity (the mean pairwise embedding distance among the 10 clips) per scale.
  2. Score the same clips with one open judge and read its preferred scale.
  3. Write down the result that would refute the project.
Plan
  1. The full sweep on two models with seeds: diversity across and within prompts, copy rate against the training set read with a proper null (a comparison with held-out items rather than a threshold), prompt adherence and an audio-language judge's score, per scale.
  2. A learned null embedding trained for one model; the same curves with it.
  3. A listening study on a balanced sample: which scale listeners prefer, against the judge's preferred scale.
  4. The paper: the curves, the recommended scale per use, and the judge-listener gap.
Result either way
Curves that fall and rise as expected give the first measured guidance trade-off for audio and a recommendation. Flat curves say guidance is cheaper than the image literature suggests, which model builders would also want to know. A judge-listener gap says automatic tuning of the scale overshoots.
What would refute it
Diversity, copy rate and judge preference do not change with the scale beyond the seed spread on either model, and listeners and judges prefer the same scale.
Paper
ICASSP 2028; ISMIR 2027.
Needs
It needs two open text-to-audio models with released weights, a public reference corpus for copy checks, one open audio-language judge, one workstation GPU, and twelve listeners for the preference check. The listening check is small and needs ethics approval.
Compute
one workstation GPU
Human study
None. The team's own listening is a check, not a study.
Main risk
Copy rate needs the training set, which is only partly public. Use the public part and report the copy rate as a lower bound.
